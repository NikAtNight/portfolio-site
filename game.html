The Premise

Every regression story opens the same way. The rules of the world change overnight. Most people keep grinding the meta that used to work, and the protagonist wins for one reason: he learns the new system's actual rules faster than anyone else.

AI is that patch, and it hit software engineering first. I don't spend energy debating whether agents take engineering jobs. They take the grinding. So I stopped competing with them at implementation and took the seat the new meta actually rewards: I run the party now.

My fleet works like any good raid. Work arrives as quests on a board. Agents are ranked, and each quest goes to the cheapest rank that can handle the judgment it needs. Nobody loots on their own word; every "done" gets verified by someone who didn't do the work. The models are commodities. The system around them is the class advantage.

The runaway fleet

One ordinary review prompt spawned 5 agents, which recursively summoned 928 more. A single session, 367 MB of transcripts. The lesson wasn't "be more careful." It was that prose constrains nothing. Delegation caps now live in config, where an agent can't talk its way past them.

Boss Fight

Forking the gateway

The open-source gateway my fleet runs on was missing its enforcement layer, so I forked it and built one: checkpointing with claim fencing (a successor can't take a quest until its predecessor's process actually exits), a fail-closed handshake between dispatcher and workers, and workspace leases handed out at dispatch instead of discovered as conflicts mid-raid.

Proof of clear — differential test sweep, branch vs base: identical failure sets, zero caused by my diff.
